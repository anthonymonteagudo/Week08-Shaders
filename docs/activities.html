Investigate, then make your own shader

Make a prediction, change one thing, inspect the result, and explain the connection. These activities begin work in class; your instructor provides any submission and grading requirements.

Lab 1 · Material investigation

Two water conditions

5:45–6:05 PM · 20 minutes. Briefing 3 min, investigation 15 min, partner comparison 2 min. Save your comparison before the break.

Create sheltered and wind-exposed versions of the same supplied material. Choose Simple, Intermediate or Advanced according to the intended motion. More complexity is not automatically a better result.

  1. In gallery bay 03, compare the four steps in one row. Open the final material instance and duplicate it twice into your own folder.
  2. Save a baseline screenshot from a fixed camera. Choose one parameter and test three values. Record exactly what changes.
  3. Use at most two controls to make the two conditions easy to distinguish. Keep color and fine texture restrained so movement is readable.
  4. Show the pair to a classmate without revealing the names. Ask what visual evidence suggests each condition.
  5. Save both instances, two captures or a short clip, and a three-sentence explanation.

Suggested controls: flow speed or distortion for Simple; height for Intermediate; amplitude/steepness for Gerstner. Use the actual names in your MI. Keep the same camera, finish and lighting for the comparison.

Optional extension: add one small contribution such as a secondary mask, controlled color variation, or an opacity option. Explain which output or material setting makes it work.

Lab 2 · Start your homework

One original shader idea

6:30–6:55 PM · 25 minutes. Briefing 3 min, then 22 min for independent work. This begins the homework; an unfinished experiment can still document a useful test.

Choose a small visual goal. Start from a supplied material, but add or change an operation or behavior that you can explain. Nodes are enough. Examples: a pulsing sign, a scanning stripe, a dissolve reveal, or your own mask combination.

  1. Keep your starting material as A. Work in a duplicate B.
  2. Make one clear change. Expose a useful parameter.
  3. Capture the result and a readable graph or code excerpt.
  4. Write about five sentences: goal, inputs, calculation, observation, next step. Use three relevant terms from class: UV, parameter, mask, Lerp, normal, blend mode, vertex offset, or Fresnel/view mask.
  5. Link any source you adapted and identify your contribution.

Keep the scope small enough to explain to a classmate. You can continue developing the homework after class.
